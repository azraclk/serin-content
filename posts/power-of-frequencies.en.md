432 Hz and 528 Hz often show up online as "healing frequencies". This post looks at what is actually supported and what is not. The short version: listening to calm music can help you relax, but there is no strong evidence that any particular frequency does something special.

## 432 vs 440 Hz: what is the difference?

Hz counts how many times a sound vibrates per second. In music everything is tuned to a reference note: the A above middle C. Most instruments today tune that note to 440 Hz. "432 Hz music" means every note is played about two percent lower.

440 Hz is not a law of nature, it is an agreement. Johann Scheibler recommended it in 1834. From the 1860s, France's 435 Hz standard was used in many countries. In 1939, delegates meeting at the BBC in London agreed on 440 Hz, ISO adopted it as a recommendation in 1955, and in 1975 it became ISO 16. Today orchestras in Europe play anywhere from 440 to 444 Hz, and Baroque ensembles use 415 Hz. So there is no "right" frequency, only a historical choice.

## Is 432 Hz proven to affect health?

A small study published in *Acta Biomedica* in 2022 tried this. Fifty-four emergency service workers were randomly split into three groups for a 10-minute break: 440 Hz music, 432 Hz music, or no music. It was double-blind.

- Anxiety scores fell in all three groups, including the group that heard no music.
- In the 432 Hz group, breathing rate fell by 2.7 breaths per minute on average and systolic blood pressure by 3.8 mmHg. Heart rate did not change significantly.
- The authors themselves note that the sample was small and from a single site.

As we read it, most of the reported comparisons are within a group. The paper does not show that 432 and 440 Hz differ from each other directly. This is a pilot study: an interesting hint, not proof.

## 528 Hz and the "Solfeggio frequencies"

528 Hz is usually named as one of six numbers called the "Solfeggio frequencies" (396, 417, 528, 639, 741, 852) and is labelled "the love frequency" or "DNA repair". The origin of this set is not ancient. According to Science Feedback, the herbalist Joseph Puleo derived them in the 1970s from a pattern of verse numbers in the Book of Numbers. The origin of the syllables do, re, mi is real: they come from the lines of an 11th-century Gregorian chant. But there is no evidence that these frequencies were used in old chants. Science Feedback also says there is no scientific or clinical evidence for the functions claimed for these frequencies.

There is a study on 528 Hz too. In a 2018 paper in *Health*, nine healthy adults (one man, eight women) listened to the same calm piano music tuned to 528 Hz and to 440 Hz, on separate days, for five minutes. After 528 Hz, salivary cortisol went down and oxytocin went up. After 440 Hz there was no significant change. The authors say the sample was limited. The study measured cortisol, oxytocin, heart rhythm and mood. It did not measure anything about DNA.

## So what does help?

The evidence we have points less to a particular frequency than to taking a break and listening to calm music. In the emergency service study above, the group that heard no music relaxed too. Listening to slow, quiet music you like for a few minutes is a more reliable start than hunting for a frequency.
