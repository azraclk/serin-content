Meditation did not start in one place on one day. This post looks at the dates that are documented and says plainly where nothing is certain. The short version: it has existed in many cultures in various forms for centuries, but the date of the earliest records is disputed.

## India: the oldest records

According to the history on Wikipedia, the earliest known records of *dhyana* (meditation) are in the Upanishads. Scholars disagree on how old which texts are: Alexander Wynne sees the earliest clear references in the middle Upanishads and the *Mahabharata* (including the Bhagavad Gita), while Gavin Flood reads a passage in the older Brihadaranyaka Upanishad as describing meditation. For that reason we do not give a firm year.

Jainism also has categories of *dhyana*. In Buddhism, meditation sits at the center of the teaching. Patanjali's *Yoga Sutras* date to about 400 CE and are noted as influenced by Buddhism.

## China: breath and "sitting and forgetting"

In China, the "Neiye" chapter of the *Guanzi* is dated to the late 4th century BCE and is called the oldest surviving writing on cultivating *qi* through breath. The "sitting and forgetting" practice in the *Zhuangzi* belongs to about the 3rd century BCE. Buddhist meditation reached China in the 2nd century CE and Japan in the 6th.

## Greek, Christian and Islamic traditions

Philo of Alexandria had written about "spiritual exercises" by 20 BCE. Plotinus had developed meditative techniques by the 3rd century. The Benedictine practice of *lectio divina* goes back to the 6th century. In Hesychasm, which developed on Mount Athos between the 10th and 14th centuries, repeating the Jesus Prayer is central. In the 12th century Guigo II introduced the term *meditatio* and the four-step ladder of reading, meditation, prayer and contemplation.

In Islam, *dhikr*, repeating the 99 Names of God, has been practiced since the 8th or 9th century. By the 12th century Sufism had specific meditative techniques such as breath control and repeating holy words.

## Spreading to the West

Practices of Asian origin began to spread in the West from the late 19th century. Today's mindfulness-based therapies grew out of a modernized version of the Buddhist idea of *sati*.

## What does science say?

Meditation is now studied. A large 2014 review in JAMA Internal Medicine looked at 41 trials with 2,993 participants. Mindfulness programs showed moderate evidence for anxiety, depression and pain, and low evidence for stress. There was no evidence that these programs were better than the other treatments they were compared with. Only 10 of the trials had a low risk of bias.

So meditation may help some people with some things. But it is not a cure-all and it does not replace treatment.
