Everyone has heard that "it takes 21 days to form a habit". But that number does not come from a study. This post looks at where it came from, what has actually been measured, and what a realistic plan for mindfulness looks like.

## Where did 21 days come from?

As the story goes, the number comes from plastic surgeon Maxwell Maltz's 1960 book *Psycho-Cybernetics*. Maltz noticed that patients took about 21 days to get used to their new faces. And he said "a minimum of about 21 days". Over time the "minimum" dropped out, and the line spread as "a habit forms in 21 days". This account comes from secondary sources, so we have not checked Maltz's book ourselves.

## What was actually measured

A study published in the *European Journal of Social Psychology* in 2010 tried to measure it in real life. 96 volunteers did a new behavior in the same setting every day for 12 weeks (84 days), such as eating fruit at lunch or running for 15 minutes before dinner. Each day they rated how automatic it felt.

- Of the 82 people with enough data, the model fitted well for 39. For them, automaticity took a median of 66 days to approach its ceiling.
- The spread between people was huge: from 18 days to 254 days. Some of the longest times were model estimates that went beyond the end of the study.
- Simple behaviors (eating and drinking) took less time and exercise took more. The difference between groups was not statistically significant.
- Missing a single day did not noticeably harm the process.

The study has been criticized too: the 66-day figure describes only the 39 people the model fitted, and what was measured was "automaticity", not "habit". So it is better to take 66 as a rough yardstick than as a fixed schedule.

## What does this mean?

Forming a habit can take much longer than 21 days and varies from person to person. Missing a day does not mean starting over. So there is no need to think "I didn't make it to 21 days, so it didn't work".

## A realistic mindfulness plan

This is a suggestion, not a proven recipe. The only things we take from the study above are that the behavior was done in the same setting every day and that missing a day did not matter.

1. Start small: a few minutes a day is enough. You can lengthen it later.
2. Pick a fixed moment: attach it to something you already do, like before your morning coffee or before bed.
3. If you miss a day, carry on the next day.
4. Do not expect results in the first weeks. The goal is for doing it to get easier.
5. Aim to keep going for a few months. 21 days is a start, not a finish line.
